AI Agents & Automation

A four-week programme that takes business professionals from a first no-code workflow to the design of a stateful AI assistant with human escalation. I rebuilt an existing course around one idea: each module is a level of automation maturity.

My role: Lead Learning Experience Designer. I restructured the inherited course, designed a new final module and five live workshops, and ran the programme through several client cohorts.

4modules, one per maturity level
70lessons, 10 minutes or less each
5live workshops, 90 minutes each
~200learners across cohorts
2languages
In short

I inherited a course organised by tools and rebuilt it as four levels of automation maturity, with a graduate profile the capstone is checked against. I designed a new final module, five live workshops and a journey map for the support team. Activation reached 63% against a 46% platform average. Task relevance scored lowest, and the next version answers that with one platform and a through-line project.

Starting point

A course organised by tools, for learners who needed to make design decisions

The previous version taught platforms one after another and ended with a vendor-specific RPA module. Learners finished with a list of tools and no rule for choosing between them. I kept the working material and changed the organising principle.

Before

Tool by tool

  • Modules named after platforms and techniques
  • A closing module tied to one RPA vendor
  • Workshops as follow-along demos
  • No shared definition of what a graduate can do
→
After

Level by level

  • Four modules, each one a level of autonomy and responsibility
  • A new final module on stateful, orchestrated systems
  • One workshop per level, built on a business case
  • A graduate profile that the capstone is checked against
Key decision 1 · Structure

The course as a maturity ladder

Every module raises two things at once: how much the system decides by itself, and how much design responsibility the learner carries. The learner always knows which level a task belongs to and what the simplest sufficient solution is.

Level 1 · week 1

Tools

Linear workflows: collect, analyse with AI, report, alert. And the point where a tool stops being enough.

Workshop: Automate Customer Feedback

Level 2 · week 2

Agents

An agent as a decision layer, grounded in company context, with a human approval step.

Workshop: Build Your First AI Agent

Level 3 · week 3

Integrations

Webhooks, APIs, structured payloads, errors and limits. How systems talk to each other.

Workshops: First AI Workflow · Decision Intelligence Simulation

Level 4 · week 4

Orchestrated systems

State, time, safety, escalation, observability. An assistant that survives delays and failures.

Capstone workshop: B2B Onboarding Concierge

Designed backwards from a graduate profile

Before restructuring I wrote a profile of the graduate as a working role: an automation owner who can design, build and launch AI-supported automation without writing production code.

Seven work functions

From choosing the automation level and describing a process as events and states, to designing human-in-the-loop checks and audit logs.

Boundaries of the role

What the graduate is not expected to do: production code, model training, turnkey legal compliance. This kept the course from drifting into engineering.

Six capstone criteria

Justified level, handled errors and delays, state and time, decision separated from execution, escalation, audit trail. The capstone is assessed against these.

Key decision 2 · New module

A new final module, and turning expert notes into lessons

Module 4 is new: 26 lessons in seven chapters on autonomy levels, state, asynchronous orchestration, tool abstraction, confidence thresholds and observability. The expert's first drafts were accurate and well structured. They read as architecture notes: each one opened with the conclusion.

Division of labour, as in my other courses: I write the brief, the expert supplies sources and technical substance, I write the final lesson, the expert reviews.

The lesson pattern I set

Step 1
A familiar work situation
Step 2
The point where the intuitive approach breaks at scale
Step 3
The shift in thinking
Step 4
Only then the concept and its name

Target reaction from the learner: "I understand why this matters and how my thinking needs to change."

How I worked with the expert

  • Written feedback that named what was right before what was missing
  • A reusable rewriting prompt with eight requirements, so the expert could redraft with an AI assistant
  • A request to mark places for visuals inside the text, for the designers
  • Quizzes written by me and reviewed by the expert for accuracy
  • An explicit note that language polish belongs to the editor, so the expert spent time on logic
Key decision 3 · Workshops

One workshop per level, with three depths inside each

Cohorts mixed managers, analysts and operations specialists. A single pace would lose half the room. Every hands-on workshop has three tracks, and the basic track is enough to reach the module deliverable.

★ Basic

The full pipeline with a simple prompt. Everyone leaves with a working result.

★★ Extended

Structured output: severity scoring, themes, parsing.

★★★ Advanced

Error handling, webhooks, alerts to a team channel.

Why a manual session sits in a course about automation

In the agent workshop, learners teach an agent to follow rules someone else wrote. Here they write the rules. Deciding which signals matter and where a person must stay in the loop is the one part of the pipeline that never happens inside a builder.

Two emails carry traps that mirror each other: one is loud and empty, one looks boring and is dated and financially binding. Teams rotate three roles: Reader, Scribe and Skeptic.

The session is the conceptual bridge into Module 4. The journey map tells the support team to expect the question "why are we not automating anything today?" and how to answer it.

Key decision 4 · RPA module

Diagnosing a module before rewriting it

The inherited RPA module was tied to one vendor and relied on features learners could not use in a real work environment. Learners formally completed RPA and could not say where it is used or reproduce it outside the sandbox. I wrote a diagnosis and a set of options before touching any lesson.

I recommended the comparative option because the course is about choosing tools and designing systems. In the rebuilt course the standalone vendor module is gone. RPA logic lives inside the workflow modules and one workshop.

Invariants first

Whatever the platform, the module must deliver three things:

  • What RPA is and how it differs from API-first automation
  • The core logic: trigger, actions, conditions, error handling, validation
  • A realistic use case that can be reconstructed and compared with alternatives

Four options, each with a fit condition

  • A. RPA as a concept, platform-agnostic
  • B. RPA as one step inside a workflow tool
  • C. A comparative module across ecosystems recommended
  • D. Keep the vendor tool, framed as a demo sandbox
Support design

A journey map that tells the support team what will go wrong, and when

The ladder has a known shape. Weeks 1–2 give fast visible wins. Week 3 is a technical trough: a no-code course suddenly has regular expressions and API documentation. Week 4 is a conceptual peak with less clicking and more designing. I wrote this down for the customer success team as checkpoints.

Setup risks named in advance

Free-tier limits, corporate approval for each tool, the heaviest workshop stack. The map says who checks what, and how many days before the session.

Reframing scripts

In week 3, error handling is the learning outcome, not an obstacle. The basic track is enough to pass.

Who is not at risk

Managers who skip build tasks but do well in design tasks fit the course profile. The map tells support not to flag them on build completion alone.

What did not work

Relevance

"Tasks are relevant to my real work" scored 3.0 of 5 in the final survey, the lowest scale. Examples were generic, and the course taught one platform while the client used another.

Two audiences

Non-technical learners found it too technical. Engineers found it shallow. Nine engineers from one team enrolled expecting agent engineering and left.

Attendance

No learner attended more than three sessions of a four-session cohort. Each workshop produced a module deliverable, so a missed session was a missed milestone.

Survey samples are small: seven to nine respondents in the final survey. I read them as signals, together with interviews run by the customer success team.
